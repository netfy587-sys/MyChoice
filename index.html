THE SPORT

A fight decided by more than power

Two fighters, matched by weight, try to land clean punches while avoiding their opponent's — using nothing but gloves, footwork, and a well-timed guard.

Despite its reputation for brute force, boxing rewards patience and precision over wild swinging. Judges score rounds on effective aggression, defence, and ring generalship — meaning a fighter who controls distance and lands the cleaner shots often wins over one who simply throws more punches.

A bout can end three ways: by decision after the final bell, by knockout (KO) when a fighter can't beat a ten-count, or by technical knockout (TKO) when a referee or a fighter's own corner stops the contest to prevent further, unnecessary punishment.

ORIGINS

From bare knuckles to the modern ring

Fist-fighting as organised sport dates back to ancient Greece, where boxing was part of the Olympic Games from 688 BCE, fought with leather thongs wrapped around the hands instead of gloves.

The modern sport took shape in 18th-century England. In 1743, champion fighter Jack Broughton wrote the first formal rules after an opponent died in the ring, introducing a 30-second count for a downed fighter and banning blows below the belt — ideas that still shape the sport today.

The version most people would recognise arrived in 1867 with the Marquess of Queensberry rules, which mandated padded gloves, three-minute rounds with a minute's rest, and a ten-second count for a knockout. Under these rules, "Gentleman" Jim Corbett beat John L. Sullivan in 1892 to become the first heavyweight champion of the gloved era.

Boxing has been part of the Olympics continuously since 1904, and women's boxing was added to the Olympic programme at London 2012, more than a century after the men's competition began.

STRUCTURE

How a bout is timed

Professional title fights run up to 12 three-minute rounds, with a one-minute rest between each. Non-championship bouts are often shorter, and amateur and Olympic-style boxing uses three or four rounds of two or three minutes.

R1–3
Fighters typically feel out range, jab, and study each other's rhythm before committing to bigger exchanges.
R4–8
The midpoint of a long fight, where fatigue starts to show and fighters look to establish control on the scorecards.
R9–12
The championship rounds — the stretch that separates a title fight from a shorter bout, testing conditioning as much as skill.

TECHNIQUE

The four punches everything is built from

Nearly every combination in boxing is some sequence of four basic punches, each thrown from a different angle and for a different purpose.

Jab

Thrown with the lead hand, straight from the guard. Fast and low-power, used to measure distance and set up bigger punches.

Cross

A straight power punch from the rear hand, travelling in a direct line — often the hardest punch a fighter throws.

Hook

A sweeping punch thrown with a bent arm, travelling sideways toward the opponent's head or body from either hand.

Uppercut

Thrown upward from a lowered position, aimed under the opponent's guard at the chin or body at close range.

JUDGING

How a fight is scored

Professional bouts that go the distance are scored on the 10-point must system, applied round by round.

10–9

The standard scorecard for a close round — the winner gets 10 points, the other fighter 9.

10–8

Awarded when a fighter dominates a round or scores a knockdown, widening the points gap.

10–10

A genuinely even round, scored equally — rare, since judges are trained to find a slight edge.

EQUIPMENT

What's actually in the corner

Boxing's gear list is short compared to most contact sports — every item is there to either protect a fighter or let them train harder without breaking down.

Gloves
Padded to protect a fighter's hands as much as their opponent's face; weight ranges from 8oz in lighter pro divisions up to 16oz for sparring and training.
Hand wraps
Long strips of cloth wound around the knuckles and wrist under the gloves, stabilising the many small bones in the hand against repeated impact.
Mouthguard
A moulded piece worn over the teeth, required at every level to absorb shock and reduce the risk of jaw injury.
Headgear
Worn in sparring and most amateur bouts to reduce cuts and impact, though most professional fights are contested without it.
Ring & canvas
A raised platform with a padded canvas floor and three or four ropes, sized so fighters have room to work angles rather than simply trade in a corner.
TODAY

Where the sport lives now

Sanctioning bodies
Four major organisations — the WBC, WBA, IBF, and WBO — each award their own world titles by division, which is why boxing often has several "champions" in one weight class until a unification bout settles it.
Olympic & amateur boxing
Governed separately from the professional ranks, amateur boxing uses shorter bouts, protective headgear in most competitions, and computerised scoring, serving as the entry point for most fighters who later turn professional.
Gym culture
Away from world titles, boxing gyms remain one of the most common places people train for fitness, discipline, and stress relief, often with no intention of ever stepping into a competitive ring.
Women's boxing
Now a full part of the professional and Olympic calendar, with its own weight divisions, world titles, and a fast-growing following since its Olympic debut in 2012.
